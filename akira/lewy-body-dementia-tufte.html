Lewy body dementia

Tufte CSS prototype — same research, different skin

Lewy body dementia is a pattern disease: the diagnosis depends on a recurring cluster of symptoms and their timing, not any one symptom alone. That is the basic Tufte fit: prose first, with the important caveat in the margin instead of a dashboard wrapper.

The disease is a neurodegenerative disease in which abnormal alpha-synuclein protein builds up in the brain. Those deposits are called Lewy bodies. They interfere with thinking, attention, sleep, movement, and sometimes perception.

The result is not just “memory loss.” It is often a mix of fluctuating attention, recurrent hallucinations, parkinsonian movement problems, and REM sleep behavior disorder.

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) means the dementia begins before or within about one year of the parkinsonism. Parkinson disease dementia (PDD) means Parkinson disease is established first and dementia appears later. That one-year boundary is not a magical biological cliff; it is a practical label switch used in diagnosis and research.

Key diagnostic indicators

The diagnosis is usually built from a pattern, not a single symptom.

Indicator What it looks like Why it matters
Dementia Decline in daily function, often with early attention, executive, or visuoperceptual problems Dementia is the central requirement for DLB diagnosis
Recurrent visual hallucinations Well-formed images or people that recur, often early One of the strongest signposts
Spontaneous parkinsonism Slowness, stiffness, tremor, shuffling, reduced facial expression, reduced dexterity A core clinical feature
REM sleep behavior disorder Dream enactment: punching, kicking, talking, acting out dreams Strong pointer to Lewy-body disease
Cognitive fluctuations Clear good/bad periods, sometimes looking delirium-like A classic clue, especially when early
Supportive biomarker evidence For example, reduced dopamine transporter uptake on DAT scan Helps when the clinical picture needs support

What does not count as enough by itself: a hallucination here, a tremor there, or a vague “confusion” label. The diagnosis is the pattern — the repeated combination of symptoms, the order they arrive in, and whether they fit a Lewy-body pattern better than another cause.

Research loop summary

Plainly: Lewy body dementia is a pattern disease. That means the diagnosis comes from a repeated cluster of problems — especially thinking changes, hallucinations, movement changes, and sleep changes — rather than from one isolated symptom.

The key divide in this loop is dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) versus Parkinson disease dementia (PDD) — same disease family, different symptom timing, different research label, different treatment framing.

This is where Tufte helps: the boundary explanation can live beside the paragraph instead of competing with a dashboard header or callout card.

What matters most is not a trivia list. It is this:

  • visual hallucinations are a major signpost
  • auditory hallucinations can matter too, but they do not diagnose anything by themselves
  • fine-motor trouble like difficulty cutting a steak is meaningful because it can reflect parkinsonism, but it is not specific on its own
  • rivastigmine has the strongest symptomatic evidence in this pass
  • donepezil is a reasonable comparator, but the evidence base is thinner
  • neither drug is a cure or disease-modifier

DLB vs PDD boundary

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) Timing rule Parkinson disease dementia (PDD)
Dementia and hallucination/attention changes show up before or around the same time as parkinsonism. About 1 year Established Parkinson disease comes first, then dementia follows later.

The boundary is useful because the syndrome label changes the trial logic and the way clinicians talk about the disease family.

Rivastigmine vs donepezil

Axis Rivastigmine Donepezil Read
Evidence in DLB/PDD Strongest in this pass Thinner and more heterogeneous Rivastigmine is the anchor drug
Symptom focus Attention, behavior, global symptomatic support Attention and hallucinations in smaller studies Both are symptomatic only
Practical use Oral or patch; patch can improve adherence Oral only Patch adds risk if the old patch is left on or two patches are used

Multi-patch medication-error risk means the patch contains real medicine, so if someone forgets to remove the old patch and puts on a new one, or uses two patches at once, the person can accidentally get too much rivastigmine. That is the part the Tufte layout can emphasize cleanly: the risk sits next to the explanation, not buried in a busy callout box.

Retitration after interruptions >3 days means that if the medicine has been stopped for more than 3 days, you do not restart at the old dose. You go back to the starting dose and build up again.
